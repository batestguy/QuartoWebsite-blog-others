The standard error, derived in three lines

Casella and Berger reach the standard error through variance algebra, before any sampling distribution exists. The repeated-samples story that most introductions lead with is an interpretation of that line, not a way to get it.
Author

Batesthommie

Published

2026-08-10

Question

You measured twenty-five things and their mean is 4.2. How far off is that 4.2 likely to be?

One number answers it, and it is not the standard deviation of your measurements.

Claim

The standard error is a distance: roughly how far the estimate sits from the truth, in the units you measured in. If the standard error of the mean is 1.3 centimetres, your 4.2 is typically off by something on the order of 1.3 centimetres.

Most introductions reach that number by asking you to imagine drawing the sample again, and again, forever, and then to take the standard deviation of all those imagined means. It is correct, and it is where a large share of readers stop, because the object being described does not exist and never will. You have one sample. You will never draw another.

Casella and Berger do not open that way. Section 5.2 of Statistical Inference derives the variance of the sample mean before it says anything about the shape of anything, and the standard error is the square root of that variance (proof). No imagined sample appears in the derivation, and none is needed to get the number.

Counter-claim

What the algebra hands you is a scale, and a scale is not a coverage guarantee.

“Your estimate is within 1.96 standard errors of the truth 95 per cent of the time” is a statement about the shape of the sampling distribution, and Section 5.2 does not deliver it. Section 5.3 does, in the normal case, and the central limit theorem does for everything else. Skip that step and the standard error is a plausible-looking number with no stated probability attached to it.

So the detour is not removable in full. It is removable from the definition, which is the part that stops readers, and it belongs after, as the proof that the number means what it was already claimed to mean.

Setup

Twenty-five measurements \(X_1, \dots, X_n\), each with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\) (assume), and independent of one another (assume). Independence is a claim about how the data were collected, not something the data can show you; it is the assumption to check before any of this applies.

Two facts do the work (proof):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(aX) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X), \qquad \operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(X_i) \ \text{ when the } X_i \text{ are independent.} \]

Casella and Berger give the second as Lemma 5.2.5. The first is the reason the constant comes out of the variance squared rather than plain.

The derivation

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^2}\, n\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \]

Read the three steps, because each one is a different fact:

  1. The \(1/n\) comes out of the variance squared — \(1/n^2\), not \(1/n\).
  2. The variance of the sum is the sum of the variances, and only because the measurements are independent. Correlated measurements do not add this way.
  3. There are \(n\) copies of \(\sigma^2\), so the \(n\) in the numerator cancels one of the \(n^2\).

The standard error of the mean is the square root, \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), and in practice you use \(s/\sqrt{n}\) (proof). That is Theorem 5.2.6(b) of Casella and Berger, with the square root taken at the end. It is three lines, it assumes no distribution, and it never mentions a second sample.

What the \(\sqrt{n}\) is doing

The standard error is a distance that shrinks with the square root of the sample size, and that exponent is the practical content of the whole derivation.

At \(n=25\) the standard error is \(s/5\). To halve it you need four times the data, not twice (proof). To cut it by ten, a hundred times the data. Cancellation has diminishing returns: the second measurement cancels a lot against the first, and the two-hundredth cancels very little against the other hundred and ninety-nine.

That sentence does more work in a study-design meeting than the formula does, and it follows from the exponent rather than from any simulation.

Where the imagined samples actually go

They are not wrong, they are redundant. The repeated-sampling picture is an interpretation of \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), and once a reader knows what the number is for, it is a good interpretation: it explains why the standard error is the scale that a confidence interval is built from.

There is also a third route, and it is the one that makes the two above look like the same thing said twice.

Efron’s bootstrap resamples the data you have: draw \(n\) observations with replacement, compute the mean, repeat a few thousand times, and take the standard deviation of those means. That is a simulation, it assumes nothing about normality, and for a mean it lands on \(s/\sqrt{n}\) times \(\sqrt{(n-1)/n}\) (proof) — the same quantity, off by a factor that is one part in fifty at \(n=25\) and vanishes as \(n\) grows.

So the three routes agree because they are estimating one number. The algebra is the exact one, the bootstrap is the one that still works when you cannot write the algebra down, and the imagined infinite population is the sentence you say afterwards to explain what either of them measured.

The theorem, run

The claim is checkable in about a second of computation. Take a population with a known mean and variance, draw four thousand samples of twenty-five from it, keep only the mean of each, and look at how far those means scatter. Nothing in the exercise is told what \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) is.

Code
set.seed(2026)
tau <- 1
sigma_pop <- 1
B <- 4000

sample_means <- function(n) replicate(B, mean(rexp(n, rate = 1 / tau)))

m25 <- sample_means(25)

sd(m25)                                  # the scatter of the means
## [1] 0.2016
sigma_pop / sqrt(25)                     # what the theorem says it is
## [1] 0.2
sd(m25) / (sigma_pop / sqrt(25))         # the check
## [1] 1.008
mean(abs(m25 - tau) <= 1.96 * sigma_pop / sqrt(25))  # not the theorem
## [1] 0.9543

The scatter comes out at 0.202 against a predicted 0.200. The ratio is 1.008, and what is left of the gap is simulation noise, not disagreement. The population is exponential — skewed hard to the right, nothing like a bell — and the scatter of the means is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) anyway, because the derivation never asked what shape the population had.

Run at three sample sizes, the square root is the visible part:

Each row multiplies the sample by four and halves the standard error — 0.202, 0.101, 0.049 against predictions of 0.20, 0.10, 0.05 — which is the exponent measured rather than asserted. Doubling the sample buys only a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\), and that is why buying precision with data is a losing trade.

The last column is the part the derivation does not supply, and it barely moves with \(n\): the interval covered the truth in 95.4, 95.3 and 95.1 per cent of runs at the three sizes. Coverage is a property of the shape of the histogram above, not of the variance algebra, and no amount of the algebra will tell you whether that shape is close enough to a bell. That is the counter-claim, with numbers attached.

What the derivation costs

  • Independence. The middle step is false for clustered, repeated-measures or time-ordered data, and nothing in the formula warns you. This is the assumption to check first, and the one most often violated in practice.
  • The coverage claim. As above, \(1.96\) needs a distribution. The standard error alone is a scale, and a scale with no distribution is not an interval.
  • Finite populations. If the sample is a large share of a small population, the standard error is smaller than \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) by the finite population correction \(\sqrt{(N-n)/(N-1)}\). At survey sizes against a national register it is invisible; in a factory with 300 employees it is not.

## The theorem, run

The claim is checkable in about a second of computation. Take a population with a known mean and variance, draw four thousand samples of twenty-five from it, keep only the mean of each, and look at how far those means scatter. Nothing in the exercise is told what $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ is.

```{r}
#| label: se-sim
#| collapse: true
set.seed(2026)
tau <- 1
sigma_pop <- 1
B <- 4000

sample_means <- function(n) replicate(B, mean(rexp(n, rate = 1 / tau)))

m25 <- sample_means(25)

sd(m25)                                  # the scatter of the means
sigma_pop / sqrt(25)                     # what the theorem says it is
sd(m25) / (sigma_pop / sqrt(25))         # the check
mean(abs(m25 - tau) <= 1.96 * sigma_pop / sqrt(25))  # not the theorem
```

The scatter comes out at `r sprintf("%.3f", sd(m25))` against a predicted 0.200. The ratio is `r sprintf("%.3f", sd(m25) / (sigma_pop / sqrt(25)))`, and what is left of the gap is simulation noise, not disagreement. The population is exponential — skewed hard to the right, nothing like a bell — and the scatter of the means is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ anyway, because the derivation never asked what shape the population had.

hist(m25, breaks = 40, freq = FALSE, col = "grey88", border = "white",
