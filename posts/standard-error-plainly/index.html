Claim

The standard error is a distance: roughly how far the estimate sits from the truth, in the units you measured in. If the standard error of the mean is 1.3 centimetres, your 4.2 is typically off by something on the order of 1.3 centimetres.

Most introductions reach that number by asking you to imagine drawing the sample again, and again, forever, and then to take the standard deviation of all those imagined means. It is correct, and it is where a large share of readers stop, because the object being described does not exist and never will. You have one sample. You will never draw another.

Casella and Berger do not open that way. Section 5.2 of Statistical Inference derives the variance of the sample mean before it says anything about the shape of anything, and the standard error is the square root of that variance (proof). No imagined sample appears in the derivation, and none is needed to get the number.

What it means in English

You took twenty-five measurements and averaged them. That average is not the truth. It is off by something, and the standard error is an estimate of how big that something usually is — 1.3 centimetres, if your measurements are in centimetres.

Four things it is not:

  • Not the standard deviation of your measurements. That describes how spread out the twenty-five were. Collect more data and it stays where it is; the standard error shrinks.
  • Not a probability. On its own it is a distance, and a distance becomes “95 per cent of the time” only once you know the shape of the scatter.
  • Not a fact about your sample. It is a claim about every sample you did not take, made from the one you did take.
  • Not something you need a second sample to compute. That is the whole point of the table below.

Code, maths, English

One row per step, read downward. Left is the R that checks the step, middle is the line as the book writes it, right is what that line says out loud. Where a step produces a number, the print follows a # — the arithmetic and the code should be seen agreeing, not taken on trust.

Code Maths English
n <- 25 \(X_1,\dots,X_n\), with \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2\) Twenty-five measurements, each with some variance. No shape is claimed for them yet.
var(2 * y) / var(y) # 4.000 \(\operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\) A constant leaves a variance squared. This is why the \(\frac{1}{n}\) becomes \(\frac{1}{n^2}\) two rows down.
var(a + b) / (var(a) + var(b)) # 1.003 \(\operatorname{Var}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_i\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\) — Lemma 5.2.5 Variances add — and only because the draws are independent. This is the one assumption in the whole derivation, and the one most often broken.
sigma^2 / n # 0.040 \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)=\frac{1}{n^2}\,n\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\) — Theorem 5.2.6(b) The derivation, all of it: pull \(1/n\) out squared, add \(n\) variances, cancel one \(n\) from each side.
sqrt(sigma^2 / n) # 0.200 \(\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) The square root of the row above. No sample was drawn a second time to get here, and none was needed.
sd(m25) # 0.201 \(\operatorname{se}(\bar X)\) should be \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) The theorem, run: 4,000 samples of twenty-five, only their means kept, then how far those means scatter. It lands on the predicted value without ever being told what that value was.
c(sd(m25), sd(m100), sd(m400)) # 0.201 0.101 0.049 \(\operatorname{se}(4n)=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{se}(n)\) Quadruple the sample, halve the error; double it and you buy only \(\sqrt{2}\). That sentence wins more design meetings than the formula does.
sd(x) / sqrt(n) # 0.263 \(\widehat{\operatorname{se}}=\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\) \(\sigma\) is never actually known, so \(s\) goes in its place. Same arithmetic, on the one sample you happen to hold.
sd(boot) # 0.256 \(\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{s^2}{n}\) Efron’s route: resample the data you already have, 4,000 times. Same number, no algebra — which is why he gets the photograph.
mean(abs(m25 - mu) <= 1.96 * sigma / sqrt(n)) # 0.954 \(\Pr\big(\lvert\bar X-\mu\rvert\le 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\big)\) Not the derivation. Coverage needs the shape of the scatter, which §5.3 and §5.5 supply, and here it reaches 95.4 per cent.
NoteWhere this comes from

The derivation. Casella & Berger, Statistical Inference, 2nd ed., §5.2 “Sums of Random Variables from a Random Sample”: Lemma 5.2.5 (the variance of a sum of independent terms) and Theorem 5.2.6(b) (\(\operatorname{Var}\bar{X} = \sigma^2/n\), p. 214). The sampling distribution of \(\bar{X}\) is §5.3; the central limit theorem is §5.5. The book does not use the words “standard error” until §10.1.4, “Bootstrap Standard Errors”, p. 478 — §5.2 stops at the variance, and taking the square root is the step every applied text adds.

The bootstrap. Efron, B. (1979), “Bootstrap methods: another look at the jackknife”, Annals of Statistics 7(1), 1–26. For the mean, the nonparametric bootstrap variance is \(\frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{s^2}{n}\), which is where the \(\sqrt{(n-1)/n}\) comes from.

## Code, maths, English

One row per step, read downward. Left is the R that checks the step, middle is the line as the book writes it, right is what that line says out loud. Where a step produces a number, the print follows a `#` — the arithmetic and the code should be seen agreeing, not taken on trust.

```{r}
#| label: se-transition
#| include: false
set.seed(2026)
options(digits = 4)

n <- 25
sigma <- 1
mu <- 1

# Stand-ins for the algebra checks: two independent vectors, and one sample.
y <- rexp(2000, rate = 1 / mu)
a <- rexp(2000, rate = 1 / mu)
b <- rexp(2000, rate = 1 / mu)
x <- rexp(n, rate = 1 / mu)

# The imagined samples, which the derivation says you never need.
m25  <- replicate(4000, mean(rexp(n,   rate = 1 / mu)))
m100 <- replicate(4000, mean(rexp(100, rate = 1 / mu)))
m400 <- replicate(4000, mean(rexp(400, rate = 1 / mu)))

# Efron's route, on the one sample actually held.
boot <- replicate(4000, mean(sample(x, replace = TRUE)))
```
